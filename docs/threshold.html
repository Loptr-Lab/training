Read for 15 minutes a day

Continue your chosen book for fifteen minutes, wherever you are in it. Afterward, jot down one observation and one connection to the project. No page quota, required purchase, account or paid membership.

A library copy, your own book, an audiobook or text-to-speech all work. Pause, take longer or miss a day without losing your place. The timer is optional; the reading list and story plan work without JavaScript.

Borrow first. Buy used if you choose.

  1. Ask your local public library. Search its catalogue, ask a librarian about interlibrary loans, and check accessible print, ebook or audiobook options. Availability and library-card requirements vary.
  2. Explore Open Library. Each book below links to an edition search. A catalogue entry does not guarantee a borrowable copy. Check borrowing help; borrowing may require an external account.
  3. Try a resale bookstore, library sale or community book exchange. Compare the edition and condition before spending. You can work with different editions; use chapter, page or audio locators from your own copy.
  4. Consider a new copy only if it suits you. An independent bookstore can help you find an edition. Amazon is an optional last resort, not the starting point or a requirement for this circle.

Start with a title you can access. There is no required purchase, affiliate link, paid membership or shared reading deadline.

Questions to help you understand and write about what you read

Choose one or two after reading. Questions and uncertainty are welcome; a reflection can be a few sentences, a voice note you keep yourself, or a sketch. You do not have to finish a chapter or answer everything.

  1. Recall: What happened in this passage, or what claim did the author make? Retell it in two sentences in your own words.
  2. Evidence: Which action, detail or example supports your answer? Record its chapter, page or audio position. Separate what the text states from what you infer.
  3. Clarify: Which word, motive or step is unclear? What does the surrounding passage suggest? What would you ask another reader?
  4. Consider another reading: Whose viewpoint are we hearing? What other explanation could fit, and what evidence would change your mind?
  5. Connect: How does this idea illuminate IVXX, restraint, the Veil or a design choice? Where does the comparison stop working?
  6. Create and reflect: What small original scene, art study or test could explore your question? What changed in your understanding after writing?

For example: “I think the character waited because ___. The detail at ___ supports that. Could ___ be another reason?” For a craft chapter: “The author claims ___. Would that still hold in this playtest situation?” Explain your reasoning rather than guessing a preferred answer.

Find your place in the reading circle

The Threshold is our shared reading practice. Start asynchronously: choose an accessible book, read for 15 minutes a day, and optionally write a question or reflection afterward. No scheduled meeting, chapter deadline or enrolment is required.

Readers bring questions. Writers can propose an original scene. Artists can make a visual study. Designers and testers can turn an observation into a rule question or a bounded test. You can participate without programming experience, and you can change roles as you learn.

When you want company, use the existing Made-Sick community entrance. Introduce the book, your reading position and one question. Opening that link does not create membership or submit your notes. A facilitator can arrange a discussion through existing community channels; this page does not book a meeting or run a live group feed.

Keep spoilers behind a clear book-and-chapter warning. Share only the notes you choose, in your own words. You can listen, pass on a question, disagree with an interpretation or take a break. There are no grades, streaks or completion gates.

How the books become part of our work

  1. Read daily. Stay with one book for 15 minutes; there is no weekly finish deadline.
  2. Bring an observation. Record its location in your edition and explain the idea in your own words.
  3. Choose the relevant story thread. Use the book cards above: IVXX and compromised identity; distributed identity and restraint; the Veil; myth in community; asymmetric perspectives; feedback and game choices; Martyr’s Boon.
  4. Make something original. Draft the small scene, two perspectives, storyboard or playtest note proposed on that card. Do not transplant a novel’s characters, plot or passages.
  5. Discuss and review. Compare the draft with the project’s current story and rule sources. Record what is accepted, revised or declined. Dominique accepts story canon; rule changes require their own review and tests.

After reading, write about your questions and thoughts if it helps you understand them. Reading-circle reflections can inform our writing and development without becoming canon automatically. The tasks on this page are concrete editorial proposals, not claims that these scenes or mechanics already exist.
