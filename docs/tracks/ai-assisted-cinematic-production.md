# AI-assisted cinematic production: a rights-aware learning path

Use this optional path to evaluate generative AI in game cinematics and visual-effects workflows without treating a vendor's tool, model, or creative assets as unrestricted.

This is a learning exercise, not an endorsement of any platform and not legal advice. Runway Academy is a vendor-authored resource for learning Runway's tools. Use it to learn techniques; use primary license terms and rights-holder guidance to evaluate permissions.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the exercise, you should be able to:

- distinguish open-source software, open-weight models, and creative materials licensed for limited reuse;
- storyboard a short original cinematic scene;
- compare a hosted AI workflow with a game-engine or Blender-based workflow and, where practical, an open-weight model;
- record source, version, license, attribution, restrictions, generation cost, and human editing effort;
- explain which rights and technical assumptions must be verified before sharing or commercial release.

## Exercise: one original 10–15 second scene

Choose a scene from an original project or invent one. Do not use another creator's protected characters, assets, prompts, or project materials unless the exact intended use is permitted by the applicable terms.

1. **Define the deliverable.** State whether you are making a cinematic proof of concept, a gameplay capture, or a playable prototype. These are different outcomes.
2. **Storyboard.** Write a short shot list with purpose, duration, camera, subject, continuity needs, and audio/caption requirements.
3. **Choose a baseline.** Use available equipment and tools first. A hosted generator may be suitable for fast visual exploration; a game engine or Blender may be better for reusable scenes; an open-weight model may be worth testing if its license and hardware needs fit.
4. **Make a small test.** Generate or render a 10–15 second original sequence. Limit retries and record the time and credits used.
5. **Compare.** If practical, make a comparable version or representative shot with another workflow. Compare visual quality, continuity, editability, repeatability, accessibility, cost, and dependency risk.
6. **Review rights and provenance.** Record the source and version of each tool, model, reference, texture, sound, and other material; the applicable license; attribution requirements; commercial-use restrictions; and unresolved third-party rights.
7. **Reflect and decide.** State what worked, what failed, what could not be reproduced, what would need permission, and whether further spending is justified.

## Rights and provenance checklist

Before sharing or publishing, answer these questions for each material component:

- What is it, where did it come from, and which exact version did we use?
- Is it code, a model, a reference, a prompt, an asset, or an output? Do not assume these share one license.
- What does the applicable license permit and prohibit, including commercial use, redistribution, attribution, and platform restrictions?
- Are third-party, likeness, trademark, music, or other rights unresolved?
- What was generated, manually edited, approved, or replaced? Can another contributor understand the production history?
- Are all public claims about permissions supported by current primary sources?

If a right or term is unclear, flag it as unresolved rather than treating silence as permission. Get qualified advice where the release decision warrants it.

## Cost and equipment record

Do not recommend a purchase from a tutorial alone. Record actual test results:

| Item | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Tool/model and exact version | Name, version, source URL, license/terms |
| Equipment or compute | Existing machine, cloud GPU, or hosted service |
| Direct cost | Subscription allocation, credits, compute, storage, transfer, tax |
| Human effort | Storyboarding, prompt iteration, editing, sound, accessibility, review |
| Output | Duration, resolution, quality issues, continuity and revision limits |
| Reproducibility | Inputs, settings, versions, workflow steps, successful rerun |
| Decision | Keep, revise, compare, seek permission, or stop |

Treat estimates as estimates and capture a dated quote before a purchase or funding request. The cheapest generation is not necessarily the cheapest production if it creates substantial repair work or cannot be revised.

## Accessibility and responsible participation

- Make the storyboard, instructions, and decision record available in readable text.
- Caption spoken content and identify meaningful sound where relevant.
- Offer a non-video route through the exercise when video generation is inaccessible or too expensive.
- Do not require participants to disclose diagnoses or private records; ask about task barriers and functional requirements instead.
- Participation should have a clear scope, time expectation, credit preference, privacy boundary, and exit path, consistent with the repository's contributor guidance.

## Completion criteria

The exercise is complete when the learner can provide:

- an original storyboard and short scene or a documented reason the test could not be completed;
- a comparison of at least two viable production approaches, or an explanation of why a second approach was not feasible;
- a cost and time record;
- a source/license/provenance checklist with unresolved questions clearly marked;
- a brief recommendation tied to the intended deliverable, not to brand preference.

## Important distinction

A cinematic sequence can demonstrate visual direction; it does not prove that a game is playable. A tool can be useful without being open source. An open-source tool does not grant rights to every asset used with it. Keep these distinctions visible in both training and production documentation.
