Your fictional task and input file

Everything below is fictional. Luma Quay, all vendors, identifiers, rates and rules are invented for this lesson.

Synthetic task prompt

Create an itinerary for two travelers, Ari and Bo, for Monday 8 June through Wednesday 10 June in fictional year F1. Include one attraction and one lunch each day, use each attraction once, and use only the supplied vendors. Both travelers require nut-free meals. Show each traveler’s total and the party total, including the shared shuttle. Budget: at most 100 credits for the party.

Synthetic input facts

  • Glass Garden: 10 credits per person; closed Monday.
  • Lantern Hall: 15 credits per person; closed Wednesday.
  • Cloud Walk: free; unavailable Tuesday (a blackout date).
  • Moss Kitchen: nut-free lunch, 5 credits per person; open all three days.
  • Ember Café: lunch, 4 credits per person; contains nuts. It cannot meet this task’s meal requirement.
  • One shuttle pass for all three days costs 20 credits for the party. Allocate 10 credits to each traveler.
  • No other costs, taxes, tips, discounts, travel-time constraints or reservations apply. Each attraction and lunch takes place within that day.

Complete fictional deliverable A

  • Monday 8 June: Lantern Hall; lunch at Moss Kitchen.
  • Tuesday 9 June: Glass Garden; lunch at Moss Kitchen.
  • Wednesday 10 June: Cloud Walk; lunch at Moss Kitchen.
  • Ari: 10 + 15 + 0 + (3 × 5) + 10 = 50 credits.
  • Bo: 10 + 15 + 0 + (3 × 5) + 10 = 50 credits.
  • Party: 50 + 50 = 100 credits, including the shared 20-credit shuttle.

Attractions: 25 per person, 50 party. Lunches: 15 per person, 30 party. Shuttle: 10 per person, 20 party. Total: 50 per person, 100 party.

1. Warm-up: the four parts

A prompt asks for an itinerary; the input file supplies prices; the response is the itinerary; the rubric checks it. Example: “Party total is exactly 100 credits” is a rubric criterion.

What is the supplied vendor price list?

Answer and takeaway

The price list supplies facts to the task; it is the input file.

Takeaway: Keep instructions, evidence, response and grading rules distinct.

2. Four principles

Atomic means one check. Verifiable means PASS/FAIL from the deliverable alone. Objective means a rule rather than personal taste. Self-contained means all grading facts are in the criterion. Example: “States the full-trip party total as exactly 100 credits, including the shared 20-credit shuttle.”

Which criterion meets all four principles?

Answer and takeaway

The exact total supplies a single observable rule and its expected value. “Delightful” is subjective; “match the file” requires an external lookup.

Takeaway: A reviewer should not have to guess what PASS means.

3. Practice A: diagnose a flawed criterion

Flawed: “Lists a nut-free lunch and states the correct party total.” Two things can succeed or fail independently. Example correction for the first check: “Lists Moss Kitchen for Monday lunch; Moss Kitchen is nut-free.”

Which principle does combining lunch and total break?

Answer and takeaway

Atomic fails: lunch choice and arithmetic are separate checks. Write a second criterion with the exact 100-credit party total.

Takeaway: Split checks that can have different outcomes.

4. Practice B: rewrite

Flawed: “The cost looks reasonable.” It uses an opinion and hides the expected value. Worked rewrite: “States Ari’s full-trip total as exactly 50 credits, including 10 credits for the shuttle.”

Which rewrite makes the party-total check objective and self-contained?

Answer and takeaway

Use the exact expected 100-credit party total and include the shuttle fact. No taste judgment or source lookup is needed.

Takeaway: Replace vague praise with a specific observable value.

5. Practice C: assign and justify weights

Positive weights reward fulfilled requirements. Negative weights penalize a defined mistake when that mistake is present. A PASS on a negative criterion triggers the penalty. Example: “Schedules any Ember Café lunch, whose meals contain nuts” has weight −6; a consistent currency label earns +1. Dietary compliance has higher stakes here. Explicit requirements come directly from the prompt or input; implicit requirements are necessary inferences, such as consistent units.

Which weighting and reason fits this task?

Answer and takeaway

Penalize the defined nut-containing meal mistake; reward consistent units modestly. Weights express task stakes, not equal-sized boxes.

Takeaway: Define the negative trigger before assigning its penalty.

6. Practice D: split without changing total weight

Flawed +6 criterion: “Lists Lantern Hall on Monday and Glass Garden on Tuesday.” Split into two self-contained checks: Lantern Hall on Monday, when open (+3); Glass Garden on Tuesday, when open (+3). The sum remains +6. Splitting allows partial credit when only one part passes.

Which split preserves the original +6 total?

Answer and takeaway

3 + 3 = 6. The other splits double or increase the weight. Preserve the total, while recognizing partial credit changes.

Takeaway: Split the requirement and preserve the weight budget.

7. Practice E: exclude an unverifiable criterion

Flawed: “The assistant checked every vendor’s live website before answering.” The itinerary does not prove an unseen browsing action. Replace process guesses with output checks: “Does not schedule Cloud Walk on Tuesday, when it is unavailable.”

What should happen to the unseen-browsing criterion?

Answer and takeaway

Exclude it from this output rubric. A process audit would need separately authorized evidence; do not infer hidden activity from polished writing.

Takeaway: Grade observable output; do not invent evidence of hidden work.

For each criterion, PASS means its statement is true. Add its weight on PASS; add zero on FAIL. Deliverable A earns 3 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 4 + 1 + 0 = 18 points. The negative criterion is a defined mistake: it fails here, so the penalty does not apply. Raw scores can be negative; this exercise has a −6 minimum and +18 maximum, not a certification threshold.

Fictional deliverable B: spot the failures

Keep A’s attractions and printed costs unchanged, but change Tuesday lunch to Ember Café. The printed Ari total is still 50, Bo 50 and party 100 credits. This is deliberately flawed: the meal violates the restriction and the printed costs no longer match its choices.

Under these eight checks, all seven positive statements remain true and the negative statement now passes: 18 − 6 = 12 points. That does not make B acceptable. This teaching rubric does not check every requirement or recalculate every possible itinerary. Add separate arithmetic-consistency and daily nut-free-lunch checks for a full evaluation. Critical requirements can also be hard gates, independent of score.

Advanced example: the existing Veiled Dominion reviewer scorecard uses weighted 1–5 dimensions and hard gates. Some broad dimensions combine judgments; they differ from the strict atomic PASS/FAIL rules taught here.

B earns 18 positive points and triggers the −6 mistake. What is the raw score?

Score answer

12 points. Subtract 6 once; do not turn a penalty into a reward.

8. Wrap-up: write three criteria

Now write your own three single-check criteria for deliverable A. Each needs a signed weight and an explicit/implicit label. Example: “States Bo’s full-trip total as exactly 50 credits, including 10 credits for the shuttle” (+2, explicit). Choose three different checks and explain their stakes.

Below, write one criterion at a time. Hints flag possible issues with wording; they do not understand meaning or decide PASS/FAIL. After one revision, use the self-review checklist and examples to improve your draft.

Write one criterion for the fictional itinerary

Targeted self-review

  • Atomic: could part pass while another fails? Split those checks and preserve their combined weight.
  • Verifiable: can you point to exact words or omissions in the response? Exclude unseen actions.
  • Objective: replace “good,” “reasonable” and similar opinions with an exact rule.
  • Self-contained: include the expected amount, date, vendor restriction or other needed fact in the criterion.
  • Weights: explain the stakes; a negative weight needs a precisely defined mistake.
  • Origin: name the prompt/input fact or explain the necessary inference.

Takeaway: a useful rubric makes a specific failure visible and supports a concrete revision.
